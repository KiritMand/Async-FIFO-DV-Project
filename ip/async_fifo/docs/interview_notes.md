# async_fifo — interview notes

Short answers to the questions this project gets asked, with the evidence for each. Each one is
meant to be said aloud in under two minutes. Numbers come from `docs/results.md` (generated by
`make results`); if they ever differ, that page is right. Paths are relative to `ip/async_fifo/`.

---

## The 60-second pitch

I verified a parameterized asynchronous FIFO, the Cummings design with gray-code pointers and
two-flop synchronizers, using only open-source tools: Verilator with UVM, and SymbiYosys for
formal.

I started from a written spec and a verification plan. The plan has 17 features, a coverage
model of 150 bins, and every property and assumption labeled with the feature it serves. A
checker script keeps the plan and the code in step.

The UVM bench has separate write and read agents on independent clocks with a randomized ratio.
The scoreboard tracks the true fill. 1,600 runs pass with every check on, and coverage closes at
100%.

The same end-to-end checker module runs in simulation, in formal and in mutation testing.
Formal proves every assertion unbounded with k-induction at two sizes. That took 25 hand-written
invariants, and PDR confirms the result independently.

Then I attacked my own checks. I broke every assertion on purpose and confirmed it fires. I
planted twelve bugs and measured which method catches each one. I ran MCY mutation testing, and
no mutant survived.

The most interesting result is a bug that nothing functional can catch: a deleted synchronizer
stage. That one needs structural CDC rules, MTBF and timing constraints, and I can walk through
why.

## How did you know you were done?

Done was defined before the work started, in vplan §7, as three measurable criteria:

- **SO-1, coverage.** 100% of the planned coverage model, merged over every seed and clock
  ratio. The model was written from the spec, not from the code. Closure found real holes: every
  test drew the same configuration for a seed, and one cross closed only by luck. Both are
  logged in `docs/coverage_closure.md`, along with how each was fixed.
- **SO-2, regression.** Zero failures over N = 200 seeds per test, with the scoreboard and every
  assertion on. N was chosen from a confidence argument: 200 clean runs catch, with 95%
  confidence, any bug that shows up in at least 1.5% of runs. Random reruns must reproduce
  their logs byte for byte, so a seed really is a reproduction.
- **SO-3, formal.** Every assertion proven unbounded at ASIZE 2 and 3, every cover reached, and
  every assumption audited: drop it and see what breaks.

Passing all three still isn't proof the checks are good, so I measured the checks themselves:
- Negative tests: every assertion fires when broken.
- The planted-bug matrix: which method catches what.
- MCY: do automatic mutations survive?

Finally, `make results STRICT=1` refuses to publish a number whose inputs have changed since the
run that produced it. So the page and the code cannot drift apart.

## Two clocks in formal, and why induction needed invariants

- **Clocks are free inputs.** SymbiYosys runs with `multiclock on`, so `wclk` and `rclk` are
  sampled at every formal step. That covers any interleaving: coincident edges, long bursts of one
  clock, ratios no simulation reaches. The covers show it, for example five write edges with no
  read edge between them.
- **Every property is written against one clock** (or the global formal step). Properties that
  span two domains are not supported. The end-to-end checker keeps its own counts of accepted
  writes and reads, so a property like "`wfull` is never optimistic" is checked on `wclk` alone.
- **Why plain k-induction failed.** It starts from an arbitrary state. That state can have
  synchronizer contents that no real run produces, such as a read pointer copy that is *ahead* of
  the real read pointer. From such a state "never optimistic" fails, although no reachable trace
  breaks it. Deeper induction doesn't help: with free clocks, the window can contain no clock edge
  at all.
- **The fix: describe the reachable states.** There are 25 invariants, for example:
  - each synchronizer stage holds an older copy of the other side's pointer;
  - the writer is never more than DEPTH ahead of what it has seen;
  - `wfull` is safe against stage 2.

  With them the proof closes at depth 6, in seconds, at every size tried. Each invariant is
  asserted, never assumed, so a wrong one fails instead of hiding a bug.
- **Cross-check.** ABC's PDR finds its own invariants and proves the spec properties without mine
  at ASIZE 2. Mine are needed for speed, not for truth.
- **Compared with ZipCPU's published proof:** stating flag safety against the synchronized pointer
  avoided the lookahead implications he needed. The FIFO-order property uses his two-value trick.
  Details: `docs/formal_notes.md`.

## Safety alone accepts a dead FIFO

Suppose `wfull` and `rempty` are both stuck at 1. That FIFO never overflows, never underflows,
is never optimistic, and loses no data, so it passes every safety property. It is also useless.

What rules it out is **bounded pessimism**, F08. Once no read has been accepted for K = 3 write
clock edges, `wfull` must be exactly "fill = DEPTH", and the mirror holds for `rempty`. K comes
from the design: two synchronizer stages plus the flag register.

The covers back it up from the other side. `cov_F08_wfull_lag_k_minus_1` shows the flag still
lagging at the edge before the bound, so K is tight and not just safe. The capacity check (F12)
shows exactly DEPTH writes are accepted from empty.

The general lesson: for every safety property, ask what trivial design satisfies it, and write
the liveness-like or exactness check that kills that design. In the bug matrix, B02 is a full
compare that raises `wfull` at fill 0. Formal catches it first on F08 and F12, before any safety
property fails.

## The MCY surprise

MCY made 300 small random changes to the RTL netlist (a bit tied to a constant, inverted, or
swapped with another) and checked whether the tests notice. I
expected survivors. There were none: every non-equivalent mutant was caught by simulation and by
formal.

The surprise came from the follow-up. I removed every assertion and ran the mutants again with
only the scoreboard and the test checks. They caught all but one.

So why have assertions at all, if the scoreboard catches almost everything?
- **Speed and locality.** With assertions on, an assertion fired first for most mutants, at the
  cycle where the bug happened. The scoreboard notices later, at the output.
- **The one exception is telling.** It is a mutant that changes the `rempty` flop's reset value.
  It is visible only while the read side is held in reset, where the scoreboard has nothing to
  compare, and only the reset-value assertion F01 sees it.

The limits matter too. Bit-level mutations cannot express a structural CDC bug like a deleted
synchronizer stage. That is why the planted-bug matrix and the CDC check exist alongside MCY.

## Metastability, and why nothing in RTL catches a deleted synchronizer stage (B08)

### The bug

B08 (`bugs/B08_one_sync_stage.patch`) makes the full compare read the first synchronizer stage,
`wq1_rptr`, instead of the second, `wq2_rptr`. The read pointer then crosses into the write domain
through one flop. That flop can go metastable when `rptr` changes close to a `wclk` edge, and its
output feeds the full-flag logic directly. A late-resolving value then has only part of a clock
period, minus the compare logic's delay, before `wfull` captures it. `wq2_rptr` is still in the
RTL, but nothing reads it.

### What the matrix measured

From `docs/bug_matrix.md` (`make bugs`). Simulation is seeds 1–20 of the seven UVM tests, 140 runs;
formal is ASIZE=2.

| Method | B08 result |
|---|---|
| Simulation, scoreboard and test checks only | 0 of 140 runs fail |
| Simulation with every assertion (white-box, e2e, `asm_`) | 0 of 140 runs fail |
| Formal bmc of the spec properties (depth 20) | no failure |
| Formal k-induction with the 25 invariants | **PASS** |
| Coverage | `cov_F08_wfull_lag_k_minus_1`: 1,866 hits on rtl-v1, **0** on B08, unreachable in formal |
| `make cdc` (structural rules) | **FAIL, rule R2**: stage 1 `wq1_rptr` feeds the compare logic |
| `make lint` / `make synth` | fail, but only because `wq2_rptr` is left unread (see below) |

I predicted that `prove` would fail on an invariant. It passes. `wfull`, computed from stage 1,
changes at the same edge where stage 2 copies stage 1, so every invariant written for the
two-stage design still holds. Formal proves the buggy design correct, because in its model it is.

### Why RTL simulation and RTL formal cannot see it

- **RTL simulation is zero-delay and two-valued at a flop.** At a clock edge a flop takes exactly
  the old or the new value of its input. There is no setup/hold window, no resolution time, no
  "in between". A synchronizer stage in RTL is just one clock of delay. Deleting it makes `wfull`
  fall one `wclk` edge sooner after a read. The flag stays conservative (it can still only lag a
  read, never lead it), so the scoreboard and every safety assertion keep passing.
- **Formal on RTL uses the same semantics.** SymbiYosys models each flop as a function of the values
  at the step before the edge. It explores every input sequence, including the clock ratios and
  phases no simulation reaches, but every one of them is metastability-free. B08 is not wrong in
  that model; it is only faster.
- **What does change is latency, and that is visible.** The flag lag bound K = 3 is no longer
  tight. The cover `cov_F08_wfull_lag_k_minus_1` (the flag still lags at the K-th `wclk` edge
  after a read) becomes unreachable. An exact-latency assertion would fail too. But that catches
  the latency change, not the CDC risk, so it is the wrong tool:
  - A safe design with an extra pipeline register after the flag logic has different latency and
    would fail the same check.
  - An unsafe single-stage design with a delay flop added after the compare keeps the latency and
    would pass.
- **Even the proof passes.** The induction invariants describe the two-stage design (for example
  `inv_wfull_safe`: wfull is safe against stage 2), yet B08 keeps them all true. Its `wfull` falls
  exactly when stage 2 catches up with stage 1. k-induction therefore proves B08 correct, and in
  the RTL model it is correct. The flaw exists only in silicon.

### What does catch it

1. **Structural CDC analysis.** Synchronizer-recognition rules on the netlist. `make cdc`
   (`common/scripts/cdc_check.py`) applies three:
   - R1: no logic on a crossing.
   - R2: stage 1 feeds only stage 2.
   - R3: each flop is reset by its own domain's reset.

   B08 breaks R2: `wq1_rptr` feeds the compare logic. Commercial CDC tools apply the same idea
   with many more rules (convergence, reconvergence, multi-bit encoding, reset crossings).
   `make lint` and `make synth` also fail on B08, but only incidentally: lint sees an unused
   signal (`wq2_rptr`), synth a lower flop count. A designer who deleted the flop completely,
   instead of leaving it unread, gets no lint warning. The flop count is specific to this design.
2. **MTBF analysis.** It turns "one stage" into a failure rate (next section).
3. **Timing constraints.** The synchronizer flops are marked `ASYNC_REG`, so the tools place each
   pair close together and leave the stage-to-stage path its full period. The gray bus gets
   `set_max_delay -datapath_only` of one source period, so no more than one pointer value is in
   flight on the crossing (AMD UG949). It also gets `set_bus_skew`, which limits how many source
   edges can launch what one destination edge captures (AMD UG903). Example:
   `constraints/async_fifo.xdc`, not run through Vivado. The trap to mention: declaring the
   clocks asynchronous with `set_clock_groups` overrides those max-delay limits.

### MTBF: how much one stage costs

For one synchronizer, MTBF = e^(S/τ) / (T_W · f_clk · f_data):
- **S** is the settling time the metastable flop gets before its output is used.
- **τ** is the flop's resolution time constant.
- **T_W** is its metastability window.
- **f_data** is how often the synchronized signal changes. Here that is the pointer update rate,
  taken as one change per source edge, the worst case.

With one stage, the compare logic sits between the metastable flop and the next capture:
S = T_clk − t_pd − t_logic. With n ≥ 2 stages, each stage-to-stage path has no logic:
S = (n − 1)(T_clk − t_pd).

`python3 common/scripts/mtbf.py --markdown` with **illustrative** values (τ = 20 ps, T_W = 50 ps,
t_pd = 100 ps, t_logic = 600 ps; these are not a characterization of any library or FPGA):

| f_clk | 1 stage | 2 stages | 3 stages |
|---|---|---|---|
| 250 MHz | 1e58 years | 1e71 years | 1e155 years |
| 500 MHz | 1e14 years | 1e27 years | 1e68 years |
| 800 MHz | 7.61 h | 1e10 years | 1e35 years |
| 1000 MHz | 0.0654 s | 2.21e+04 years | 1e24 years |

Sensitivity at 800 MHz (`--fclk-mhz 800 --tau-ps <τ>`), as at a slow process, voltage or
temperature corner:

| τ | 1 stage | 2 stages | 3 stages |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 ps | 1e9 years | 1e35 years | 1e85 years |
| 20 ps | 7.61 h | 1e10 years | 1e35 years |
| 40 ps | 0.0293 s | 26.6 h | 1e10 years |

How to read it:
- **MTBF is exponential in the slack.** At 250 MHz even one stage looks safe on paper. Near the
  clock's limit, the logic delay eats the settling time, and one stage fails every 65 ms at 1 GHz,
  while two stages last about 22,000 years.
- **Designs don't rely on the slow case.** τ grows at low voltage and temperature: doubling it
  turns two stages at 800 MHz from 10¹⁰ years into a day. A chip has many synchronizers, and the
  requirement is set by units shipped × lifetime, not by one part on a bench. The second stage
  buys margin across corners; a third is added at high clock rates or for long lifetimes.
- **Use real numbers before quoting one.** Replace τ and T_W with vendor characterization; FPGA
  tools report synchronizer MTBF from their own data.

### One-line answers

- *Why two flops?* Stage 1 may go metastable. Stage 2 gives it a full clock period, with no logic
  in between, to resolve before anything uses the value. MTBF grows exponentially with that time.
- *Why gray code?* A synchronizer captures each bit independently. When at most one bit changes
  per source edge, any capture is the old or the new pointer, never a mix. B07 sends the binary
  pointer instead: in RTL it passes every end-to-end check, and only the source-side one-bit
  assertion (`ast_F09_wptr_cdc_one_bit`) fails.
- *Why can't simulation find metastability bugs?* RTL flops have no timing window; every capture
  is clean. Metastability is a physical effect. It is handled by structure (CDC rules), statistics
  (MTBF) and constraints (timing), not by functional checks.
